Long read · Week of October 23, 2026

The Market Just Split in Two. You Get to Pick Your Side.

Rates are up, special servicing just hit a 13-year high, and lenders are taking buildings back. Apartments and warehouses are also filling faster than they have in years. Here's what both markets mean for you this week.

You opened your phone this week to two completely different real estate markets.

In one, the 30-year mortgage is above 7%, the Fed is hiking again, and the share of commercial loans sitting with special servicers is the highest it's been since 2013. Lenders are taking back office towers. A mall in upstate New York has turned into a courtroom fight with a town board standing in the middle of it.

In the other, apartments are filling faster than at any point since 2021. Warehouse leasing just had its best quarter in four years. Companies are signing big office leases again, and buyers are writing checks for buildings at prices nobody would have accepted two years ago.

Both markets are real. They're happening at the same time, sometimes on the same block. Your job this week is to know which one you're standing in, and what it's going to cost you to stay there.

Here's what you need to know.

1. What money costs you right now

Start with the number that touches every deal you'll look at this year.

Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed averaged 7.32% this week. That's down from 7.40% the week before, and it's the first weekly drop in nine weeks. Don't let the dip fool you. Rates just touched their highest level since November 2023, and a year ago that same loan cost you 6.19%. The 15-year sits at 6.68%.

The reason is simple. The 10-year Treasury, which mortgage rates follow, has spent most of October above 5%. Closing yields ran between roughly 5.06% and 5.24% from October 9 through October 21.

And the Fed is no longer on your side. On September 16, the Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75% to 4.00%. It was the first hike since 2023, and the vote was 12 to 0. Chair Kevin Warsh put it plainly: "Inflation is too high and has been for too long." The Fed now projects 2026 PCE inflation at 3.7% and doesn't see a return to its 2% target until 2029.

The next decision lands Wednesday, October 28, and the market expects the Fed to hold. As CNBC put it, "Everyone expects the Fed to hold rates in October." Don't read a pause as relief, though. Most Fed officials projected at least one more hike this year, and several banks expect the next increase in December.

What this means for you:

  • If you're underwriting today, stop assuming rate relief in year one. Build your deal so it works at today's cost of debt and treat any cut as upside.
  • If you have a loan floating or maturing in the next 12 months, your refinance math changed this fall. Run it again this week, not next quarter.
  • If you're a buyer with cash or low leverage, high rates are thinning out your competition. That's an edge. Use it.

2. The dislocation, in plain English

"Dislocation" gets thrown around a lot. Here's what it actually means for you.

Most commercial loans written from 2019 through 2021 were sized for a world of 3% money and full buildings. That world is gone. When those loans come due now, the property is often worth less than the debt, and the cost of a new loan eats whatever cash flow is left. The owner can't refinance. The lender doesn't want the building. Everyone is stuck at a price nobody agrees on.

That gap between what the loan says and what the building is worth today is the dislocation. It's where the pain is. It's also where the discounts are.

Look at two deals that closed in just the last week.

In downtown Los Angeles, a group of lenders sold the loan on the Wells Fargo Center South Tower, a 45-story Bunker Hill office building, for about $115 million. The original loan was $290 million. That's roughly 40 cents on the dollar, or about $100 a square foot for a skyline tower.

In Fort Lauderdale, a Boca-based investor bought 200 East Broward Boulevard, a 21-story office building, for $69.7 million. The sellers paid $81.5 million in 2017. That's a loss of nearly $12 million and a new basis of about $296 a square foot.

Somebody lost money on both of those trades. Somebody else just bought in at a price that can work at today's rates. You want to be the second person.

3. Special servicing just hit a 13-year high

When a CMBS loan gets into trouble, it moves from the regular servicer to a special servicer. Think of it as the ICU. The special servicer decides whether to extend, modify, foreclose, or sell.

According to Trepp, the CMBS special servicing rate hit 11.49% in September. That's the highest reading since February 2013 and up 84 basis points from a year ago.

Here's how it breaks down by property type:

Another $2.65 billion across 42 loans moved into special servicing in September alone. Office made up 57% of it. The biggest names on the list tell you this isn't only a story about tired suburban buildings:

  • One SoHo Square, Manhattan: $512.8 million, transferred over an expected maturity default even though payments are current
  • Starwood regional mall portfolio: five malls, 3.7 million square feet, $436.9 million
  • 51 West 52nd Street, Manhattan: $420 million
  • Apple Sunnyvale office loan: $209 million

Now look at the other side of that same report, because most people skip it. $1.95 billion across 48 loans left special servicing in September, up 237% from August. Eighteen of those loans went back to normal servicing. Thirty, worth $953.5 million, were paid off or liquidated.

Liquidated means sold. Sold means somebody bought. The pipeline is moving, and every resolution puts an asset in front of a buyer.

Watch this one too. In Philadelphia, the borrower behind 2400 Market asked for its own $205.4 million loan to be moved to special servicing ahead of a December maturity. The building is nearly full and has beaten its underwritten cash flow. When a good building with a good rent roll still can't refinance cleanly, you know the problem is the debt, not the real estate. Those are the situations you want to be tracking.

4. The REO battles you should be watching

Once a lender decides to take the property back, things get messy. These fights show you how lenders, borrowers, courts, and even local governments are going to behave over the next two years.

Eastview Mall, Victor, New York

This one is turning into a case study. Owner Wilmorite took out a $210 million mortgage in 2012, when the mall was assessed at $350 million. Wilmorite now values it at $112 million. The loan went to special servicing back in 2022, got two extensions, and came due on September 6. It wasn't paid. The lenders filed for foreclosure within days and are waiting on a court-appointed receiver. Rialto Capital is the special servicer.

Here's the twist. The Town of Victor and Wilmorite have floated a plan to take five mall parcels through eminent domain and transfer them to a separate Wilmorite-owned entity, with the lenders paid market value. Lender counsel called it a "debt evasion scheme." Wilmorite fires back that Rialto has collected close to $10 million in fees over three years while blocking money for basic operations. The town says the mall supports about 4,000 jobs and roughly 20% of the town budget. A two-month public hearing window opened September 28, and a court challenge looks likely.

Why you care: if local governments start using eminent domain to help owners keep assets away from lenders, every lender in the country will price that risk into how fast they foreclose and how they sell.

Illinois Center, Chicago

A 2.1 million square foot office complex on a $239.48 million loan that has been with special servicer LNR Partners since April 2024. It defaulted at its 2025 maturity. A foreclosure auction is set for November 12, and LNR is positioned to bid on behalf of the bondholders, pending court approval. If that's how it plays out, the property becomes REO and the real sale comes later. Put it on your calendar.

2100 Ross Avenue, Dallas

A $78.4 million CMBS loan on an Arts District office tower. The loan wasn't paid off at maturity, the property went through a foreclosure sale, and the trust now owns it. That's a fresh REO asset in one of the strongest job markets in the country.

1166 Avenue of the Americas, Manhattan

A lender started foreclosure on August 14 against office condo units tied to a $110 million mortgage. Real estate attorneys are now arguing in public that foreclosure has become an expensive last resort for distressed office, and that negotiated workouts often save more value. Expect to see more of both.

The pattern: lenders are done extending the worst loans, but they're still slow and careful about selling. That stretch after foreclosure and before a clean sale is where a prepared buyer gets a phone call returned.

5. Vacancy: the honest picture

You'll hear "office is dead" and "office is back" in the same week. Neither one is true.

Cushman & Wakefield's third quarter numbers put national office vacancy at 19.8%. That's high. It's also down 30 basis points from a year ago. Net absorption has been positive for five straight quarters. Sublease space has dropped for ten straight quarters and is now under 89 million square feet. San Francisco, the city everybody wrote off, cut its vacancy by 410 basis points in a year.

Now the caution. Third quarter absorption was only about 49,700 square feet nationally, per Cushman & Wakefield. That's barely positive. The trend is pointing up, but it's crawling.

The part that matters most for your next five years: new office supply has almost stopped. Developers delivered just 7.1 million square feet through the first three quarters of 2026, the lowest total in more than 30 years. When nothing new gets built, the good existing buildings get leased. The weak ones get converted, sold cheap, or knocked down.

So vacancy isn't one number anymore. It's two markets. Top-tier space in strong locations is tightening. Older, commodity space in weak locations is where that 16.85% special servicing rate lives.

6. Now the good news, and there's a lot of it

If all you read were the distress headlines, you'd miss the strongest demand signals this market has shown in years.

Apartments are filling up

National multifamily vacancy fell to 8.6% in the third quarter, the third straight quarterly drop. Renters absorbed nearly 350,000 units through the first nine months, up 14% year over year and the best pace since 2021. At the same time, construction is drying up. Fewer than 480,000 units are underway, the lowest count since 2015. Average asking rent is $1,950, up 2.3% from a year ago. Dallas-Fort Worth alone absorbed more than 50,000 units. San Francisco rents are up nearly 16%.

What that means for you: more renters plus fewer new buildings equals pricing power for owners over the next two to three years. That's why a distressed multifamily loan today is a very different bet than a distressed office loan.

Industrial just had its best leasing quarter in four years

JLL tracked 202.6 million square feet of warehouse leasing in the third quarter, up 38.5% from a year earlier. Year-to-date absorption of 231.6 million square feet has already passed all of 2025. National vacancy is 6.8% and falling. Rents are up 3.2% to $10.55 a square foot. The demand is coming from logistics firms, construction materials, data center supply chains, advanced manufacturing, and defense. In Dallas-Fort Worth, industrial vacancy dropped a full 100 basis points in a single quarter.

The money is coming back

Industrial investment hit $95.3 billion year to date, up 38% and the second-highest total on record. CBRE reported this week that its U.S. property sales revenue jumped 37% in the third quarter. Buyers are back at the table. They're just paying today's price, not 2021's.

Companies are signing for the long haul

Morningstar just signed more than 275,000 square feet at Chicago's Thompson Center, which Google is redeveloping. It's the largest downtown Chicago office lease announced this year, with move-in planned for late 2028. That's a company betting on downtown for the next decade. Manhattan, San Francisco, Orange County, Dallas, and San Jose led the country in office demand last quarter.

Homebuyers are still showing up

Existing home sales rose 1.5% in September to a 3.99 million annual pace, even with mortgage rates over 7%. The median price is $417,500, up 1.3% from a year ago. Inventory is 1.6 million homes, a 4.8 month supply. That's more choice for you as a buyer than you've had in years, and prices that are holding steady instead of running away from you.

7. What you should do this week

Here's how to turn all of this into action.

  1. Re-run every deal at today's rates. Use a 10-year near 5% and a 30-year near 7.3%. If the deal only works with a rate cut, it doesn't work.
  2. Know your maturities. If you own anything with debt due in the next 18 months, call your lender now. Borrowers who show up early with a plan get extensions. Borrowers who show up at maturity get a special servicer.
  3. Follow the resolutions, not only the defaults. Every loan that leaves special servicing through a sale is an asset that just hit the market. Track the liquidations. That's your deal flow.
  4. Separate the building from the debt. A nearly full building with a bad loan is a financing problem, and the right basis solves it. A half-empty building in a weak location is a real estate problem. Price them differently.
  5. Lean into the demand. Multifamily and industrial fundamentals are improving while some of their owners are still stuck with 2021 debt. That combination is the sweet spot.
  6. Put these dates on your calendar.
    • Oct. 28: Fed decision
    • Nov. 12: Illinois Center foreclosure auction
    • Mid-November: Trepp's October special servicing report
    • December: a wave of office maturities, including The Gateways in Newark ($285 million) and 2400 Market in Philadelphia ($205.4 million)

The bottom line

You're not looking at a crash, and you're not looking at a recovery. You're looking at a reset. The debt is getting repriced loan by loan, building by building. The owners who borrowed at the top are handing assets back. The buyers who show up with patience, fresh capital, and realistic numbers are getting the best entry points this cycle has offered.

That's the market you're in this week. Where distress becomes opportunity.

Article 01 · Tender Offer · Valuation · Tax Strategy

The Company Says It's a Great Valuation.
But Is It Great For You?

You're being told this is a strong price. The email says "attractive valuation," "employee liquidity opportunity," "limited window." It feels like a win. You've been waiting for this moment for years.

But here's what nobody in that email tells you: you're evaluating the headline price. You should be evaluating the after-tax outcome — and your remaining concentration risk once the dust settles.

Your Situation — Real Example

You own 20,000 shares. Tender offer price: $25/share. Total value: $500,000. You're allowed to sell 50%, so you think: "I'll take $250K off the table." Makes sense. Here's the math you're skipping:

Your basis is $5/share. The gain is $20/share. On 10,000 shares, that's $200,000 of taxable gain. Federal capital gains at 20%, plus Net Investment Income Tax at 3.8%, plus California state tax at roughly 10% — and your $250K becomes $160K–$170K after tax. And you still hold $250K in paper value tied to one company.

What Happens If You Do Nothing Strategically

You sell randomly (or not at all). You don't model the taxes. You keep most of your exposure. You overestimate your liquidity. You remain concentrated. And if the valuation drops before the next round — you've lost both the paper gains and the diversification window you missed.

If valuation drops after this window → you lose the gains AND the missed diversification. Both.

Misaligned Interests — The Real Problem

The company wants a clean tender, a validated valuation, and happy employees who participate. You want real liquidity, tax efficiency, and risk reduction. Those are not aligned objectives. Institutional investors on the other side of this deal have a full team — legal, financial, tax. You have the summary deck they prepared for you.

What To Do Instead

1
Model after-tax proceeds first — not the headline number. What actually hits your account after federal, NIIT, and state taxes? That's your real decision basis.
2
Set a target concentration limit — ask yourself: what percentage of my total net worth should remain in this single company? If the answer is under 30%, adjust your sell amount accordingly.
3
Treat it like an institutional trade — partial exit, risk-managed hold, reallocation plan ready before you sign anything.

Independent advice isn't paranoia. It's table stakes. The window is typically 20–30 days. The tax structure of how you receive proceeds can vary significantly based on grant type (IRC §83, §1221). Model it before the deadline, not after.

Article 02 · RSU · ISO · AMT · Equity Sequencing

The Type of Equity You Hold
Determines Everything.

You think: "It's all stock comp." It's not. RSUs and ISOs behave completely differently — and if you sequence them wrong, you can create a six-figure tax problem without knowing it.

Your Situation — Real Example

You have RSUs vesting this year worth $300,000. You also have 10,000 ISOs at a $2 strike, with current fair market value of $20. What most people do: they let RSUs vest (taxed automatically), then exercise ISOs in the same year because "it's time." Here's what actually happens:

The $300K in RSUs is taxed as ordinary income. Federal plus California comes to roughly 40–50%. That's $120K–$150K in tax. Then the ISO spread — $18/share on 10,000 shares — creates $180,000 of Alternative Minimum Tax income. You just triggered AMT on top of ordinary income in the same year. Total incremental tax bill: often $100,000+.

The Critical Difference

RSUs are a tax event you don't control — they vest, you owe, full stop. RSUs are taxed as ordinary income at vesting (IRS Pub 525). ISOs are a tax event you do control — you choose when and whether to exercise. ISOs can trigger AMT on the spread between strike and FMV at exercise (IRC §56–§57). The spread at exercise is AMT income even though you receive no cash.

RSU income + ISO AMT stacked in the same year = the most common six-figure mistake we see.

The Sequencing Rule

1
Accept RSU income as fixed — plan around it, not with it. Withholding often underestimates your true tax. Reconcile before year-end.
2
Run AMT projections before ISO exercise — calculate your AMT exposure in the year you plan to exercise. Use lower-income years strategically.
3
Sequence ISOs across tax years — exercise gradually, or consider early exercise (83(b) election) when the spread is still small and FMV is low.
4
Start the holding period clock deliberately — ISOs get capital gains treatment if held 1 year post-exercise AND 2 years from grant date. The clock only runs if you act.

Same company. Same total compensation. Two employees — one pays $80K in tax, one pays $200K+. The difference is not intelligence. It's sequencing. Nobody explains this because equity comp advisors and tax advisors rarely sit in the same room.

Article 03 · Pre-IPO · Lockup Strategy · Wealth Realization

You Built Wealth on Paper.
Now Don't Lose It in Execution.

You're heading toward an IPO. Your equity is worth $1M+, maybe more. And you're thinking: "I'll just hold. It's going higher." The problem isn't your conviction. The problem is that you don't control lockups, market conditions, or post-IPO volatility — and those three variables are what determine whether your paper wealth becomes real wealth.

What Actually Happens at IPO — Real Example

Equity value at IPO pricing: $1.2M. Lockup period: 180 days. IPO price: $30/share. During the lockup, the stock drops to $22. You're still locked. Your $1.2M is now $880K on paper — and you can't do anything about it. Six months of watching matters you can't control.

The lockup expiration date is your first real decision point. Plan before it arrives — not after.

The 3-Bucket Strategy

1
Liquidity Bucket — Sell enough to cover taxes on the equity income event AND fund 1–2 years of life. This is not optional. If you under-withhold, the IRS will find you. Size this bucket first.
2
Core Hold Bucket — Maintain meaningful upside exposure to the company you helped build. Set a price target or time horizon. This is intentional, not passive.
3
Diversification Bucket — Systematically redeploy into uncorrelated assets: index funds, real estate (see below), fixed income. This is how paper wealth becomes a portfolio.

The Tax Stacking Problem

IPO year is often the highest-income year of your life. RSUs vest at IPO price. Options exercise creates spread income. State bonus withholding kicks in. The combination can push your effective rate above 50% in California. Planning the sequence of what vests, when, and how it's taxed can save real money — but only if you act before the window, not after.

You're not "selling early." You're converting risk into optionality. The upside remains. The downside is limited. And you stop being fully exposed to a single outcome you can't control.

Article 04 · Merger · Equity Conversion · All-Stock Deal

You Don't Own
What You Think You Own.

Your company got acquired. Your equity converted. And you're assuming: "It's basically the same." It is not. In an all-stock acquisition, every parameter of your equity award changes simultaneously — and the merger documents describing those changes are written for lawyers, not for you.

Your Situation — Real Example

You had 5,000 options at a $5 strike. Conversion ratio in the all-stock deal: 0.5. Now you have 2,500 options at a $10 strike. Your share count is cut in half. Your strike has doubled. And the spread — which drives your AMT exposure — is completely different. Before: FMV $25, spread $20. After conversion: FMV $25, strike $10, spread $15. Your AMT calculation has changed materially, and you didn't get a memo.

Three Mistakes We See Repeatedly

1
Modeling AMT against the old strike — not the adjusted one. The AMT is calculated on the spread at exercise. If you're using pre-merger numbers, you're modeling the wrong tax liability.
2
Assuming old vesting still applies — Vesting schedules can be modified at the close of a deal, buried in the "treatment of equity awards" summary rather than your standard grant doc. Read it.
3
Waiting for the IPO filing — By the time the S-1 is public, the optimal planning window is gone. The pre-filing period is when strategic options are open. After filing, the blackout clock starts.
What to do this week: Get the conversion ratio in writing → Recalculate strike, spread, share count → Model AMT on the new numbers → Understand the blackout start date.

Rebuild From Zero

Step 1: Confirm conversion math — ratio, new strike, new share count — in writing from HR or the plan administrator. Step 2: Recalculate the spread. This drives the AMT calculation. Step 3: Re-evaluate your exercise timing and sale sequencing against the new numbers. Step 4: If there's an IPO coming, map the decision windows before the blackout, not during it.

The planning window in a post-merger pre-IPO situation is narrow. The employees who act before the S-1 filing have options. The ones who wait until it's "clear" are working with whatever remains.

Article 05 · Concentration Risk · Asset Basket · Real Estate · LA Tech Workers

Half Your Net Worth Is
One Company's Stock.

You came in doing everything right. Maxing your 401k. No consumer debt. Saving aggressively. Smart, disciplined. And yet: 60% of your total net worth is in the equity of a single company — the same company that pays your salary, your health insurance, and your bonus. That's not a portfolio. That's a concentrated bet on a single counterparty, three times over.

The Math of Concentration

Net worth: $2M. Company stock: $1.2M. Diversified assets: $800K. If the stock drops 50%, your net worth drops to $1.4M. You just lost $600K. And unlike a diversified portfolio, there's no other leg of the stool to hold you up — because your income, your equity, and your savings are all moving in the same direction at the same time.

A concentrated position isn't a reward. It's a risk that hasn't been repriced yet.

The 5-Layer Asset Basket for LA Tech Employees

1
Liquidity layer (10–20%) — 6–12 months of cash, separate from your emergency fund. This is the buffer that lets you make strategic decisions instead of reactive ones during a downturn.
2
Tax-advantaged base — 401k maxed, backdoor Roth if income limits apply, HSA if eligible. These are the closest things to guaranteed after-tax return that exist in the tax code.
3
Real assets (real estate) — Primary residence equity, West LA multifamily, or REITs. Real estate provides depreciation offset against your ordinary income, which is significant when your W-2 income is in the top bracket. (See our underwriting model below.)
4
Diversified equity (not your employer) — Index funds, sector exposure outside tech. The goal is non-correlation with your employment income. If the tech sector corrects and you lose your job, this layer should not be moving in the same direction.
5
Equity optionality (your company stock) — Intentionally sized, not defaulted to. Set a target: under 30% of net worth in a single position. Use liquidity events to systematically reduce toward that target.

The Tax Angle — Why Real Estate Belongs in This Basket

If you're making $400K–$700K in W-2 income plus RSU income in a high year, your marginal California rate plus federal is above 50%. Real estate depreciation directly offsets ordinary income — $50K in depreciation on a $1.5M property can reduce your tax bill by $25K+ per year. It's one of the few remaining tax shelters available to high earners without complex structures.

Article 06 · Behavioral Finance · Selling Psychology · Equity Realization

$800K on Paper Feels Different
Than $800K in Your Account.

You have $800K on paper. And you hesitate to sell. Here's why, exactly: selling makes it final. If you sell and the stock goes to $1.2M, you were wrong. If you hold and it goes to $300K, at least you didn't make an active mistake. The brain treats selling as a decision you can be judged for — and holding as something that just happened to you.

That asymmetry is a cognitive trap. And it's expensive.

Not Selling Is Also a Decision

Example: Stock goes from $800K to $500K during a correction. You didn't sell. You lost $300K of real opportunity — a down payment on a Venice Beach fourplex, two years of runway to start something, a diversified portfolio that would still be whole right now. The "no decision" still cost you $300K.

The freeze is the most common wealth destruction event we see. Inaction is a position.

The "Sell Enough to Make It Real" Framework

1
Define a concrete use for the proceeds — not "diversification" in the abstract. A specific down payment. A specific debt paid off. A specific investment. Make the money do something visible before you sell it.
2
Size the sale to fund that concrete thing — not everything. Not a round percentage. Just what it takes to fund the thing you identified. Keep the rest. You haven't abandoned the upside; you've carved out a floor.
3
Set a tax-aware timeline — partial sales can be sequenced across tax years, or structured using covered calls or collars if you want to limit downside without triggering a sale event in the current year.
4
Hold the rest with an explicit thesis — not inertia. If you're holding because you believe in a specific outcome at a specific time horizon, that's a position. If you're holding because selling feels irreversible, that's the freeze. Know which one it is.

The shift is from "all or nothing" to "partial realization plus upside retention." Some of it becomes real. The rest keeps working. You stop living in between — neither invested nor committed — and start making intentional decisions with real money.

RealFiModel Research · Due Diligence · June 2026
The $200K Investor
Beats the $2 Billion Fund.

A $2 billion fund and a $200K investor are doing the same underwriting on a multifamily deal. Same rent roll. Same T12. Same cap rate math. One of them has a team of 12 analysts, two law firms, and a committee that meets on Thursdays. The other closes in three weeks and gets the deal.

This isn't a story about the little guy getting lucky. It's about what real due diligence actually looks like.

This is why the investor willing to get their shoes dirty consistently outperforms the one waiting for the deck to be perfect.

Should you even start with price? Here's the instinct most investors have: see a deal, look at the asking price, and immediately decide if it's worth pursuing. If the number feels high, they move on. That's backwards.

Price is an output, not an input. The question isn't "is $1.1M too much for this property?" The question is "what is this property actually worth to me, given what I know that the market doesn't?" The moment you anchor to asking price, you've handed your underwriting over to the seller. You've let their number frame your analysis before you've even looked at a single unit.

Start instead with what the asset can do. What does the rent roll look like versus market? What are the actual operating expenses — not the pro forma the broker emailed you, but the real ones? What's deferred? What's broken? What's the story the numbers aren't telling? Then, and only then, does price become a conversation.

This weekend: dumpster diving in the sub-$1M market. We spent this past weekend doing what we call dumpster diving — working through the lower end of the opportunity stack, specifically deals under $1 million. It's unglamorous work. Most of what you find is priced wrong, tired, or structurally complicated in ways that aren't immediately visible from the listing.

But two deals stood out. Both under a million. Both worth a second look. And one of them — completely unexpectedly — may have been one of the more interesting discoveries we've made in months.

The fire damage wasn't what it seemed. One of the two properties had noted fire damage. I already knew this going in — it was actually one of the reasons it made the list. Distressed properties often create pricing gaps that informed buyers can exploit. Fire damage is scary on the surface, which means less competition, which sometimes means better terms.

But when we got there, something didn't add up. The damage was concentrated entirely at the rear of the structure. That alone is unusual — fires typically follow air flow, fuel sources, and structural pathways. Rear-only concentration is worth noting. But then two more things became clear: the damage predated the actual fires the notes were presumably referencing. And the neighboring properties were completely intact.

Take a second with that. If this were an accidental fire, or even a fire related to the broader incident referenced, you'd expect some evidence of spread, some scorch on adjacent structures, some pattern consistent with the story being told. There was none.

What we may have stumbled onto — and it took a while to set in, because it was genuinely unexpected — is something you rarely find when you're looking for it and occasionally find when you're not: a property with a damage story that doesn't hold together. Which means the pricing may be discounting something that either didn't happen the way it's described, or happened in a way that changes the remediation calculus entirely.

What the model tells you, and what it doesn't. At realfimodel.com, we built the underwriting tool around a simple reality: the model is a gauge, not a gospel.

When you pull in a rent roll, the platform surfaces market-level expense benchmarks — insurance ranges, maintenance ratios, management fee norms for the submarket. It gives you a starting framework fast, which matters when you're moving through a high volume of deals.

But here's what every experienced investor knows and every new investor learns the hard way: nothing beats first-hand. The model tells you what expenses should look like. The neighbor walk tells you what's actually happening. The drive-by on a Tuesday evening tells you who's living there, how the property is being maintained, what the street feels like at dusk.

No algorithm captures the overgrown lot next door, the boarded unit two doors down, or the landlord across the street who tells you the owner hasn't been seen in eight months. The model gets you to the shortlist. Your feet get you to the deal.

The edge is in the discomfort. The $2 billion fund doesn't go dumpster diving. They don't drive canyons on weekends.
